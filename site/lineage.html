Lineage

Quasar is modelled on the SOMA Laboratory Pulsar-23, a semi-modular drum machine, and runs on the M-VAVE FM-1, a small groovebox with a JieLi chip. This page says what was taken from the Pulsar-23, how it was rebuilt, and where the FM-1 made it give way.

The instrument

The Pulsar-23 (SOMA Laboratory) is an analogue synthesizer and generator of rhythmic patterns that SOMA calls "organismic": four sound generators — a bass drum, a bass, a snare and a hi-hat — among 23 modules and 13 auxiliary units, all patchable. A clock and a binary divider array drive four looper-recorders; envelopes, a controlled chaos generator (SHAOS), an LFO, an FX processor, a distortion, VCAs, inverters, switches, attenuators and single components for live circuit bending can be joined with cables, and with fingers on touch sensors. It is played as much as it is programmed.

Quasar does not copy it. Nothing of SOMA's firmware, schematics or sound data was used — none of it is public. The engine was written from the published descriptions: SOMA's manual, the Synthdawg Pulsar-23 notebook, reviews, forum posts and the FAQ. It reproduces the instrument's architecture and the behaviour those sources describe, then goes beyond it toward harder, more broken sounds.

The path through it

As Quasar plays it: steps and keys strike six tracks, each one of the Pulsar's four circuits in one of its modes; every voice passes Quasar's own drive and WRECK stages; the mix goes through the FX processor, the stutter, the parallel distortion and a soft clip. The modulation the Pulsar does with cables, Quasar does inside each sound: four routes and four macros.

The circuits as modelled

Block diagrams of the four sound generators as the engine computes them. They are drawn from this firmware's code, not from SOMA's circuits: where the sources say what a stage does but not how, the stage is this engine's own design. Every range on them is this engine's choice.

BD

An integrator and comparator make a triangle; a pitch modulator, charged at every strike, bends it down from as much as four octaves above. DRIVE shapes the triangle toward a sine, then a square. MOD × AMT is linear in Hz per volt, as on the instrument. OMG! and WTF? — the Pulsar's two "circuit-bending" inputs — are modelled as currents injected into the core and the modulator, which is a guess at what they do, not their real bend points.

BASS

A table-free recursive oscillator, y = sin(φ + β·ȳ): as SHAPE raises the feedback the sine becomes a saw, then crossfades to a band-limited square. WARP is an asymmetric saturator; the low-pass is a state-variable filter whose band stage saturates, so it sings at high resonance. In PRC mode an impulse and noise strike three resonators instead of the oscillator.

SD

Pink noise and a pitched "spectral" noise — an oscillator on the note, swept by band-limited noise — mixed, then a resonant band-pass whose level holds as Q rises. The clap, a burst of one to four hits a few milliseconds apart, sounds on accents.

HHT

White noise held at a clock that follows the note — the Pulsar's "TUNE" of its hat — then WARP, an expander that thins it into sparse grains, and a resonant high-pass that can ring on its own.

Module by module

Every module of the Pulsar-23 exists in Quasar's engine and is tested on the computer. Not all of them can be reached from the FM-1's panel: the Pulsar's patching is done with 3.5 mm cables, and the FM-1 has no jacks to patch. The engine keeps a virtual patchbay (136 pins, 32 cables), but Quasar plays the instrument with its normal, unpatched routing and gives each sound internal routes in place of cables.

Close to the instrument

  • The signal model: pins on a 0–10 V scale, module outputs carrying the instrument's +5 V bias, audio inputs AC-coupled; joined outputs mixing passively; envelopes taking the strongest voltage on their trigger.
  • The four sound generators' topologies as described: the BD's triangle core and pitch modulator, the BASS's feedback oscillator and saturating low-pass, the SD's two noises and band-pass, the HHT's resampled noise and high-pass.
  • SHAOS's published sequence lengths (63, 16 and 217 steps), its 1-bit, 2-bit and sample-and-hold outputs.
  • The LFO's three ranges and shape control, the divider array, the clock's 128-tick, four-bar loop.
  • The FX processor's three modes and MAD!, its delay and hall, and its clock slowed by up to seven times — with its pitch and bandwidth falling with it.
  • The parallel distortion after the mix.

Where it gives way

The FM-1 is a small instrument with a small chip: a JieLi AC79 with two 32-bit cores, no floating-point unit, about 32 KB of fast on-chip memory for the code that runs every sample and under 100 KB for everything the firmware keeps. Quasar's engine is fixed-point integer arithmetic throughout, split across both cores. On the device, all six tracks playing 16ths take about 40 % of the processor.

Digital models, not circuits

The Pulsar-23 is analogue where it counts. Quasar computes models of what the published sources describe. No component values, transfer curves, the BD and BASS firmware, the SD noise circuit, the HHT WARP circuit, the SHAOS taps or the FX programs were available, so all of those are this engine's own. It has not been compared by ear with a real Pulsar-23, and no acoustic equivalence is claimed.

No cables

The FM-1 has no patch points and no touch sensors. Quasar plays the Pulsar's normal routing; the patching moves inside the sounds as four routes each (sources: SHAOS, the LFO, the dividers, noise, the FX outputs, any track's output or envelope) and four macros. The utility modules that only make sense with cables are modelled but silent.

Time in blocks

To fit six voices in the processor, an unpatched voice renders 32 samples (0.73 ms) at a time, so its gate is read once a block. A route's source reaches the voice one block late, and is read every fourth sample for MOD, DRIVE and VCA; at most 12 distinct sources are heard at once. Patched nodes would run sample by sample, as on the instrument.

Percussive by design

Every envelope falls once its attack peaks, gate held or not, and release is capped near a 0.22 s time constant. That suits a keyboard full of hits and keeps voices from costing processor time after they die. There are no drones, by choice.

Different on purpose

  • Six tracks, each any circuit, instead of four fixed voices.
  • Pitch from the note played: there is no TUNE knob, so drums follow the scale.
  • The FX send is after the track's level (on the instrument it is before), so turning a track down turns down its echoes too.
  • The Pulsar's bus is mono; the FM-1 keeps each track's pan.
  • The clock follows the session tempo; the looper-recorder is replaced by a 16-step sequencer with parameter locks, patterns and sessions.

Beyond it

What Quasar adds, all of it written for this firmware: twelve further circuit modes; a per-voice drive; WRECK (fold, ring, crush, bits) on every voice; 147 sound families drawn at random; macros and internal routes; a stutter on the whole output; a phrase generator with polymeters and Euclidean layers; ten BREAK ways; and a PERFORMANCE mode that moves every track at once and puts everything back.

The other lineage

Quasar's control layer — the step sequencer, patterns and sessions, MIDI, the screen and the installer — comes from Bigorna, the same author's FM-1 firmware after the Elektron Digitone II, itself built on Tonitor, Felucca and SLOOP, with Melodee's dual-core start. None of Bigorna's sound engine is in Quasar: the audio is new.
